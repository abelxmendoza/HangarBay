The mission

Spend less time fixing your setup.
More time building robots.

Robotics development is heavily centered on Linux. MacBooks are excellent portable workstations, but combining Linux-oriented robotics frameworks, simulator versions, virtual machines, and graphics settings can turn a first project into days of troubleshooting. Darkstar aims to make that path easier without pretending the underlying complexity doesn't exist.

01 / COMPATIBILITY

Known-good environments

Make ROS, Gazebo, Ubuntu, and PX4 version choices explicit instead of guessing which combinations work.

02 / DIAGNOSTICS

Preflight before failure

Check prerequisites and explain what is missing, why it matters, and how to recover.

03 / EVIDENCE

Prove what ran

Keep live state, recorded results, demos, and unknowns clearly labeled.

From the founder

Why I built Darkstar

I've loved MacBooks since reading Steve Jobs' biography and spending years using iPhones and MacBook Pros. They're thoughtfully designed, lightweight, portable, and powerful enough to work from almost anywhere—even while reclining comfortably with a laptop.

When I started learning ROS 2, PX4, Gazebo, and their surrounding stacks, the experience wasn't nearly as seamless. The robotics ecosystem largely assumes Linux, and I spent hours navigating VM setup, version conflicts, terminal commands, and obscure failures. At one point, even the browser inside my Ubuntu VM broke until I changed a Wayland-related graphics/session setting.

I built Darkstar so the next MacBook user learning robotics doesn't have to fight through all the same problems. The goal isn't to replace Linux; it's to make Linux-first robotics tools easier to use from a Mac.

Design principle: Preserve engineering transparency. Simplify setup, surface real diagnostics, and never confuse a demo with verified execution.
